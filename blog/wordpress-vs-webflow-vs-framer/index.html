WordPress vs Webflow vs Framer: Which Is Best for a Business Website?

Every comparison article you find is written by someone who sells one of the three. I build on all of them, so here is the version without a thumb on the scale.

Almost every comparison of these three is published by a company that sells one of them. That is worth knowing before you read any of them, including this one — except that I build on all three, so I have no particular outcome to protect. What follows is the conversation I actually have with clients, written down.

The short answer

If you want the decision without the reasoning:

  • WordPress if you need real ecommerce, deep integrations, a large or growing content operation, or full ownership of your hosting and data.
  • Webflow if you want design precision and a marketing team that publishes without a developer, and you are happy inside its feature set.
  • Framer if the site is small, motion matters more than structure, and you want it live this week.

If none of those obviously describes you, the rest of this is worth reading, because the wrong choice here is expensive to reverse.

What each one actually is

They get compared as if they are the same category of thing. They are not.

WordPress is open-source software you install on hosting you control. It powers a very large share of the web, and its strength and its weakness are the same thing: it will let you do almost anything, including a great deal you should not.

Webflow is a hosted visual development platform. You design in a canvas that produces real HTML and CSS, with a CMS behind it. You do not own the platform, and you cannot leave it without rebuilding.

Framer began as a prototyping tool and grew into a site builder. It is the fastest of the three to get something attractive and animated online, and the most constrained once a project grows.

WordPress

Where it wins

  • Ecommerce with actual complexity. WooCommerce handles product variations, tax rules, shipping logic and payment gateways that the other two simply do not attempt.
  • Integrations. If it has an API, something already connects it to WordPress. CRMs, booking systems, ERPs, membership platforms, multilingual setups.
  • Content at volume. Hundreds or thousands of posts with editorial workflow, roles and permissions is exactly what it was built for.
  • Ownership. Your database, your files, your hosting. Nobody can change the pricing of software you already have.

Where it hurts

  • It is yours to maintain. Core updates, plugin updates, security, backups. Real, ongoing, and the single most common reason a WordPress site quietly rots.
  • Plugin sprawl. Every plugin is code someone else wrote, loading on every page. Twenty of them is how a simple site becomes a slow one.
  • Page builders can wreck performance. Elementor and its peers make a site editable by non-developers, at a cost in markup weight that has to be actively managed.

Most of the WordPress work I am hired for is fixing exactly these three things on sites somebody else built. That is not an argument against WordPress — it is an argument for building it carefully. I have written more about that on the WordPress development page.

Webflow

Where it wins

  • Nothing to maintain. No plugin updates, no security patches, no server. That removes an entire category of ongoing cost and risk.
  • Design fidelity. You are working with real CSS concepts, so what the designer drew is what ships, including the details.
  • Publishing without a queue. Marketing writes a page and it is live the same day. No deploy, no ticket.
  • Fast hosting by default. CDN and SSL are handled, which removes most of the technical obstacles to good Core Web Vitals.

Where it hurts

  • Ecommerce is basic. Fine for a simple catalogue. Not fine for variations, complex tax, or subscription logic.
  • CMS limits are real. Item caps and nesting constraints will find you eventually on a large content site.
  • You cannot take it with you. Exporting gives you static files, not your CMS. Leaving means rebuilding.
  • Cost scales oddly. Site plan plus workspace seats adds up in a way that surprises people.

The failure mode I see most is a Webflow project dragged together without a class naming convention — hundreds of one-off classes nobody can untangle. That is avoidable, and it is what the Webflow development process is designed to prevent.

Framer

Where it wins

  • Speed to live. A good-looking marketing site in days rather than weeks.
  • Motion. Genuinely the best of the three for scroll and interaction work without hand-written code.
  • Low floor. A designer can ship without a developer at all.

Where it hurts

  • It gets constrained quickly. Beyond a landing page or small marketing site you start fighting it.
  • Thin CMS. Workable for a blog. Not for a structured content operation.
  • Animation has a weight cost. The thing it is best at is also the thing most likely to hurt your Core Web Vitals if nobody is watching.

The five questions that actually decide it

  1. Who updates this in six months? If the answer is a non-technical colleague, that rules out anything needing a developer for routine changes.
  2. Are you selling products, and how complicated are they? Variations, tax rules or subscriptions means WordPress. A handful of simple items does not.
  3. How much content, and how fast is it growing? Fifteen pages is a different problem from fifteen hundred.
  4. Does anything need to talk to anything else? Your CRM, booking system or inventory. Check this before you choose, not after.
  5. Do you need to own it? Some businesses genuinely cannot depend on a hosted platform's pricing and roadmap. Most can.

What I would pick

Local service business, 8–15 pages, wants to edit text themselves: Webflow, or WordPress if a booking or CRM integration is involved.

Online shop with real product complexity: WordPress and WooCommerce. Not a close call.

Funded startup that needs a marketing site now and will rebuild in a year: Framer, deliberately treated as disposable.

Agency or consultancy publishing regularly: WordPress if the content operation is serious, Webflow if design consistency matters more than volume.

Established business with an ageing site and existing rankings: whichever you choose, the migration matters more than the platform. Get the redirects wrong and you lose traffic you already paid for — see redesigning without losing rankings.

Are any of them better for SEO?

Not meaningfully, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling something. All three can produce clean markup, fast pages and correct metadata. All three can be built badly enough to rank for nothing. The platform sets your ceiling; the build determines whether you get near it. What actually decides SEO outcomes is site structure, page speed, content quality and technical hygiene — none of which is a platform feature. That is the argument in full on the SEO services page.

The honest conclusion

The platform matters less than the two things nobody asks about: who maintains the site, and whether it was built with any structural discipline. I have seen fast, well-organised sites and slow, unmaintainable ones on all three.

Pick for the constraint that will still be true in two years — usually who is editing it and whether you are selling anything complicated — then have it built properly.
