How to Hire a Solo WordPress Freelancer (Without Getting Burned)

The reviews on a freelancer's profile tell you what happened. They rarely tell you why. Here is what to check before you pay anyone anything.

ON THIS PAGE

  3. Questions worth asking before you pay anything
  4. Contract basics that protect both sides
  5. How pricing should be structured

Every completed contract on my Upwork profile is rated 5.0, and I still tell people that star ratings are the least useful signal in this whole process. A rating tells you the client was satisfied at the end. It does not tell you how much of that satisfaction the client had to manage themselves — chasing updates, catching mistakes, rewriting a vague brief three times because the first version got misread. The checklist below is built from the other side of that: what actually predicts a smooth project, not what looks reassuring on a profile page.

Start with a written brief, not a call

A fifteen-minute call feels productive and produces almost nothing you can hold anyone to. Write down what you are building, what pages or features it needs, what platform it should be on if you have a preference, your real deadline, and your budget range. Send that before the first conversation, not after.

Two things happen when you do this. First, you find out fast whether a freelancer reads carefully — a reply that ignores half your brief is a preview of how the project will go. Second, any quote you get back is actually comparable to another freelancer's quote, because they priced the same scope, not two different guesses at what you meant.

What to actually check in a portfolio

  • Live sites, not just screenshots. A screenshot cannot show you load speed, mobile behaviour, or whether the site still works two years after handoff. Open the actual URL.
  • Work that resembles yours. A beautiful portfolio site is not evidence someone can build a WooCommerce store with variable shipping rates. Look for the specific type of build you need.
  • Recency. WordPress, page builders and hosting all move. A portfolio that has not been updated in three years tells you nothing about current practice.
  • What they say about the constraint, not just the result. Case studies that mention what was hard and how it was solved are worth more than ones that only show the finished screen — see how I write mine on the portfolio page for the kind of detail worth expecting.

Questions worth asking before you pay anything

  1. "Who exactly will be doing the work?" Some profiles advertised as solo freelancers are actually small outsourced teams. Neither is wrong, but you should know which one you are hiring.
  2. "What happens if I need changes after launch?" Get this in writing before you start — an hourly rate, a support retainer, or nothing at all. Finding out after the fact is how relationships sour.
  3. "Can I see a site you built more than a year ago, still live?" This is the single best question for separating a build that was engineered to last from one that just needed to demo well at handoff.
  4. "What is not included in this quote?" Stock photography, copywriting, ongoing hosting, a logo — a lowball quote often just excludes items a higher quote included.
  5. "What is your process if you get sick or overbooked mid-project?" A solo freelancer with no answer to this is a real risk on anything with a hard deadline.

Contract basics that protect both sides

You do not need a lawyer for a typical business website, but you do need this in writing, even as a simple email confirmation:

  • Scope, listed as specific pages and features — not "a full website."
  • Total price, or the rate and an estimated hour range if hourly.
  • Payment milestones tied to deliverables, not just dates.
  • What counts as a revision versus a change request that costs extra.
  • Who owns the code, content and design files once you have paid in full — this should always be you.
  • A rough timeline with the understanding that content delays on your side push the date, which is normal and should be stated up front rather than discovered later.

How pricing should be structured

A fixed quote for a clearly scoped project protects you far better than an open hourly rate, because it puts the estimation risk on the freelancer, not you. Reasonable freelancers price fixed-scope work in milestones — commonly a third up front, a third at a build milestone, and the remainder at launch. Be cautious of anyone asking for full payment before starting, and equally cautious of anyone unwilling to take any deposit at all; both are unusual enough to be worth a direct question.

If your scope is genuinely undefined — you are exploring, not ready to commit to a spec — hourly is honest and fine. Just get an estimated range before work starts, so an open-ended rate does not become an open-ended bill.

What good communication looks like

Not speed. A reply within a working day, every time, beats an instant reply that is inconsistent. What actually matters: does the freelancer tell you about a delay before you have to ask, do they flag a problem the moment they find it rather than at the deadline, and do they push back when your request will hurt the site rather than silently doing whatever you asked. That last one is a genuine signal of experience — someone who has shipped enough sites has opinions worth having, and will tell you when yours is a mistake.

If you are trying to decide between a freelancer and a full agency for this project, the freelancer-vs-agency comparison covers the structural trade-offs. And if your business is small enough that you are not sure what you even need yet, start with what a small business site actually requires before you request quotes at all — it will make every conversation after that shorter and more accurate.
