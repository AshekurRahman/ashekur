How Much Does WordPress Maintenance Cost? A Realistic Breakdown

Plenty gets written about what it costs to build a WordPress site. Almost nothing gets written about what it costs to keep one running — until something breaks and the answer becomes urgent instead of theoretical.

See how much a custom website costs for what it takes to build one. This is the number that comes after that — what it costs to keep it running, secure and working once it's live, which is a real, ongoing cost most people budget for badly or not at all.

What “maintenance” actually includes

Before the numbers mean anything, the scope needs to be clear, because “maintenance” gets used loosely. At minimum, a real maintenance plan covers: core, plugin and theme updates — ideally tested on staging before going live, not applied blind to production — automated off-site backups, and uptime monitoring with alerts. Better plans add malware and vulnerability scanning, a periodic performance check, and some allotment of small changes or support hours each month.

What it is not: an open-ended retainer for new feature development. Building a new page, a new integration or a redesign is project work, priced separately — maintenance keeps what already exists running, it doesn't grow it.

The real cost of doing it yourself

Doing it yourself costs nothing in cash and something real in two other currencies: time, and risk. The time cost is straightforward — someone has to actually log in, check for updates, run them, and confirm nothing broke, on a schedule, indefinitely. The risk cost is less visible until it isn't: a plugin update applied directly to a live site with no staging test and no recent verified backup is a real, if usually small, chance of an afternoon spent firefighting a broken checkout or a blank homepage instead of running the business.

DIY is a legitimate choice for a low-stakes brochure site with an owner comfortable in wp-admin. It stops being the obvious choice the moment the site takes payments, generates real leads, or would cost real money to be down for a day.

Basic plans, typically $50–100/month

This tier covers the essentials: updates tested on staging first, daily off-site backups, and uptime monitoring with alerts. What's usually not included at this price: malware scanning, a performance check, or any allotment of support hours for small changes — those are typically the next tier up. This is the right level for a straightforward site where the owner just wants the update-and-backup cycle handled reliably and doesn't need much beyond that.

Mid-tier plans, typically $150–250/month

Everything in a basic plan, plus malware and vulnerability scanning, a periodic Core Web Vitals or speed check, and a couple of hours of small changes included each month — a text edit, a broken link, a plugin setting, without a separate invoice for every minor request. This is the tier most small businesses running an active site actually need: enough coverage that a problem gets caught before a customer finds it, without paying for a response-time guarantee they don't need.

Priority plans, typically $300–500+/month

Everything in the mid tier, plus a faster guaranteed response time (often same-day), more included hours, and usually a monthly report or a short call rather than just a summary email. This tier makes sense for a site where downtime or a slow fix has a real, immediate cost — an active WooCommerce store, a lead-generation site during a paid campaign, or a business that simply can't tolerate a multi-day wait if something goes wrong.

What actually drives the price

Between providers, the same handful of factors move the number regardless of who's offering the plan:

  • Site complexity — a WooCommerce store with dozens of plugins costs more to maintain safely than a five-page brochure site, because there is simply more that can break on an update.
  • How critical uptime actually is — a site that loses real revenue for every hour it's down justifies a faster response-time guarantee than one that doesn't.
  • Response-time SLA — same-day or priority response costs more than standard, because it requires the provider to hold capacity in reserve.
  • Whether included hours roll over — a plan where unused hours are lost each month is priced differently from one where they bank.
  • Agency overhead vs. a solo freelancer — an agency plan frequently starts higher for comparable scope, since it's carrying more overhead than a single person handling the same work directly.

The cost of skipping it

The honest comparison isn't "maintenance cost vs. zero." It's maintenance cost vs. the real, if irregular, cost of skipping it: a hacked site needs a proper clean-up rather than a monthly fee, and that's before counting the downtime or the reputational cost of a Google blacklist warning while it's resolved. A compatibility break from an untested update can take a WooCommerce checkout offline during exactly the wrong week. And time spent firefighting an emergency is time not spent running the business, at a moment that wasn't chosen. None of that is guaranteed to happen in any given month — but averaged over a few years, it very often costs more than the maintenance plan that would have caught it early would have.

Annual vs monthly billing

Most maintenance plans, including mine, offer a discount for paying annually rather than month to month — commonly somewhere in the 10–20% range, since it's less administrative overhead for the provider and better cash flow up front. It's worth asking about specifically if you're already confident you'll keep the plan for a full year; if you're not sure yet, starting monthly and switching once you are costs little beyond the discount itself.

How to decide what you actually need

  • A simple brochure site, comfortable in wp-admin, low stakes if something breaks — DIY is genuinely fine, as long as backups are actually verified occasionally.
  • Anything taking payments, generating real leads, or with content that changes regularly — at least a basic plan, so updates are tested rather than applied blind.
  • An active store, a campaign-dependent site, or multiple people with admin access — a mid-tier or priority plan, where the response-time guarantee and included hours actually get used.
