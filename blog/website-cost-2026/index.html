How Much Does a Custom Website Cost in 2026? A Breakdown by Platform

There is no single number that fairly describes what a website costs. Here is what actually drives it on WordPress, Webflow and custom frontend builds — and how to get an accurate one for yours.

“How much does a website cost?” is the wrong first question. The right one is “what does it need to do, and who’s maintaining it after launch?” There is no single number that fairly describes “a website” — cost depends on scope, design complexity, functionality, integrations, CMS or platform choice, how much content there is, and technical complexity. Here is what actually moves that number across the platforms I build on — WordPress, Webflow, and custom React/Next.js — without a made-up price tag attached to any of them.

Why website pricing is all over the place

Search “website cost 2026” and you will get wildly different answers, because the question is really three questions stacked together:

  • Platform. WordPress, Webflow, and a custom-coded frontend have different cost structures, not just different price tags. See WordPress vs Webflow vs Framer if you have not settled on one yet.
  • Scope. A five-page brochure site and a fifteen-page site with a blog, CMS, and gated content are not the same job even on the same platform.
  • Who maintains it. A site your marketing team can update themselves costs differently over time than one that needs a developer for every change.

Below is what actually drives cost on each platform, plus the cost most quotes leave out entirely: what happens after launch. If you would rather answer a few questions than read the whole breakdown, the free WordPress cost calculator gives you an instant starting estimate using the same logic.

What actually drives the cost, whatever platform you choose

Before the platform-specific detail, the factors that move cost regardless of what you build on:

  • Design complexity. A fully custom design implemented pixel-for-pixel from Figma takes longer than adapting a well-built existing template, and it looks like it too.
  • Content volume and structure. Fifteen pages with a simple structure is a different job from a site with multiple content types, filters, and relationships between them.
  • Integrations. A CRM, booking system, payment processor, or inventory feed each add real scope — and each needs to be identified before a quote, not discovered halfway through the build.
  • E-commerce complexity. A handful of simple products is a fraction of the work of a catalog with variants, subscriptions, or multi-currency pricing.
  • Performance and SEO work. Getting a site fast and getting it structured to rank are real line items, not something that happens automatically because the site is new.
  • Ongoing maintenance needs. Whether the site needs a developer on call or can be run day-to-day by a non-technical team changes the total cost of ownership even when the initial build price is similar.

WordPress

What moves the cost on a WordPress build:

  • Custom theme built from a Figma design costs more than adapting a well-built existing theme — but it is the difference between a site that looks like everyone else’s and one that does not.
  • WooCommerce stores scale with catalog complexity: a small catalog with simple shipping is a fraction of the work of a large one with variants, subscriptions, or multi-currency.
  • Speed and Core Web Vitals work is often quoted separately, and it should not be treated as optional — a slow WordPress site loses both rankings and conversions no matter how good the design is.

Ongoing considerations: hosting quality, plugin licenses, and either a maintenance retainer or a developer on call for security patches. WordPress’s flexibility is also its ongoing responsibility — it is the platform most likely to break from a bad plugin update if nobody is watching it. See WordPress development for how that work is scoped.

Webflow

What moves the cost on a Webflow build:

  • Figma-to-Webflow builds are priced on design complexity, not page count — a pixel-accurate build of a highly custom design takes longer than a simpler one with more pages.
  • CMS structure matters more than people expect. A well-planned CMS — collections, fields, relationships — is what lets a marketing team publish blog posts or case studies without touching code. A poorly planned one means every new content type needs a developer.
  • Interactions and animations are a real cost driver in Webflow specifically — they are one of its strengths, but every custom interaction is time on the clock.

Ongoing considerations: hosting is bundled into the platform and there is no plugin ecosystem to maintain, which is generally why total cost of ownership over a few years runs lower than WordPress — you are trading some build-time flexibility for less ongoing maintenance risk. See Webflow development for how that trade-off plays out in practice.

Custom Frontend (React / Next.js / Vue)

What moves the cost here, and this is where “it depends” is the most honest answer, because custom frontend work is not really “a website” — it is an application interface, even when it looks like a marketing site:

  • Whether there is a design system already. Building to an existing component library is faster than establishing one from scratch.
  • Data and API integration. A largely static Next.js marketing site is a lighter build than a product interface pulling from multiple APIs with authentication, dashboards, and real-time data.
  • SEO requirements. Next.js can be built server-rendered and perform well in search, but that has to be a decision made at the start of the build, not bolted on afterward. A client-rendered React app with no server-rendering strategy is expensive to retrofit for search visibility later.

Ongoing considerations: usage-based hosting, and a developer relationship for any future change — there is no visual editor a non-developer can use, which is the fundamental trade-off against Webflow or WordPress. See Frontend development for the kind of interfaces this usually means.

The cost nobody quotes: what happens after launch

Every platform has a real answer to “what if we need to change something in eight months,” and it should factor into the platform decision as much as the build price does:

 Small content editNew page or sectionNew feature

WebflowClient can edit CMSLow-medium, within the design systemDeveloper time
Custom frontendDeveloper timeDeveloper timeDeveloper time

A marketing team publishing weekly needs WordPress or Webflow. A product team shipping an application interface needs custom frontend regardless of the build cost, because “can my team edit this” is not the relevant question for a product UI.

What a fair quote actually looks like

There is no one-size-fits-all price for a custom website, and any quote that hands you a single number without a scope breakdown is worth questioning on any platform. A fair quote separates:

  • Design, or design implementation if you are supplying Figma files
  • Build — pages, CMS setup, integrations
  • Performance and SEO work
  • What is included in the price versus billed as a change request afterward

That is also why a fixed quote only works once scoping happens first — a fixed price on an undefined scope is a fixed price on someone’s guess. I provide a fixed quote once we have actually defined what the project involves, not before. If you want a reference point before that conversation, see current starting prices.

How to get an accurate number for your project

What actually produces a real number, fast, is a short scoping conversation covering:

  • Roughly how many pages or content types the site needs
  • Whether a non-technical team needs to edit it after launch
  • Any systems it needs to talk to — CRM, booking, payments, inventory
  • Whether e-commerce is involved, and roughly how large the catalog is
  • Examples of sites or designs you like, if the design is not settled yet

Bring that to the conversation and the range narrows within one call, rather than staying vague across a dozen quotes from a dozen agencies answering a question they were never given enough detail to answer accurately.

The short answer

  • Brochure site, marketing team needs to publish content themselves: Webflow or WordPress.
  • E-commerce store: WordPress/WooCommerce for flexibility, with cost scaling to catalog complexity.
  • Product interface, dashboard, or app-like frontend: custom React/Next.js, and budget for an ongoing developer relationship, not just the build.
  • Content-heavy site that also needs to rank: whichever platform, budget separately for the SEO and Core Web Vitals work — it is not free with the build.

If you are collecting quotes and the platform has not been discussed yet, that is usually a sign the scoping conversation has not happened either — and neither has an accurate number.
