Freelance WordPress Developer vs Agency: Which Should You Hire?

Both build WordPress sites. The difference is not skill — it is structure, and structure decides your cost, your timeline, and who picks up the phone in month six.

ON THIS PAGE

  2. Where a freelancer wins
  3. Where an agency wins
  4. Cost, honestly
  5. The hybrid nobody mentions
  6. How to decide, in one afternoon

I have sat on both sides of this. Four years employed — agency and product teams — before going solo full-time in 2024, and Upwork client work running alongside that the whole way through. So when someone asks whether they should hire a freelancer or an agency for their WordPress site, I do not have a side to sell. I have a structural answer instead, and structure is what actually matters here, not the marketing on either side's homepage.

What you are actually buying

A WordPress build is design, development, content migration, and then months of small requests after launch. An agency sells you a team — a project manager, a designer, one or more developers — coordinated through a process built to scale across many clients at once. A freelancer sells you one person doing all of it, with no coordination layer between you and the work.

Neither is inherently better. They are different answers to the same question: who is doing the thinking, and how many people have to talk to each other before something gets built.

Where a freelancer wins

  • Price. No project manager, no account handler, no office overhead baked into the rate. You are paying for build time, close to directly.
  • Speed of decisions. A question I ask myself gets answered immediately. A question that has to go through an account manager to a developer and back adds days, not because anyone is slow, but because that is what a chain of handoffs costs.
  • Continuity. The person who built your site is the person who answers when something breaks eighteen months later. Agencies rotate staff; the developer who built your site in year one is frequently gone by year two.
  • Direct accountability. There is no one else to blame and no one else to credit. If a freelancer says something will work, that is a personal commitment, not a sales promise made by someone who will not be doing the work.

Where an agency wins

  • Capacity. A large multi-site rollout, a hard deadline that needs several people working in parallel, or a project that genuinely needs a designer, a developer and a strategist at once — one person cannot parallelise that.
  • Redundancy. If your one freelancer gets sick, has a family emergency, or simply gets overbooked, an agency can reassign. A solo freelancer is a team of one; plan around that, or explicitly ask how they handle it.
  • Formal process. Larger organisations sometimes need the paper trail — SOWs, change orders, a procurement-friendly entity — that an agency is built to produce and a sole trader may not be set up for.
  • Breadth under one roof. Brand strategy, paid media and web development from one vendor, if you specifically want that bundled rather than coordinated by you across separate providers.

Cost, honestly

Agency WordPress builds commonly run from the low five figures upward once you include discovery, project management and a design phase billed as its own line item. A solo freelancer doing comparable scope — same platform, same functional requirements — is typically somewhere between a third and two-thirds of that, because there is no coordination layer being billed alongside the build time. See the full cost breakdown by platform for actual numbers rather than ranges.

The gap is not because freelancers are cheaper labour. It is because an agency's rate has to cover people who are not writing code — account managers, sales, office costs. That is a legitimate cost if you need what it buys. It is dead weight if you do not.

The hybrid nobody mentions

The option most comparison articles skip: an experienced freelancer who has actually worked inside agencies and product teams brings agency-grade process without the agency markup. I run projects with a written brief, a fixed quote before work starts, and staged milestones — the same discipline a good agency uses, minus the layer of people whose job is to relay information between you and the person actually building the site.

This does not scale to a twelve-person rollout with a six-week deadline. It scales fine to the overwhelming majority of business websites, which is most of what actually gets built.

How to decide, in one afternoon

  1. Write down your actual scope. Not "a nice website" — page count, whether you need e-commerce, what integrates with what, and your real deadline.
  2. Check whether it needs parallel work. One developer, working sequentially, is fine for almost all single-site builds. If you genuinely need three workstreams running simultaneously to hit a date, that is an agency-shaped project.
  3. Decide how much you value continuity. If "who fixes this in eighteen months" matters to you, weight that toward a freelancer with a track record, not the cheapest quote you receive.
  4. Ask both for a written process, not a pitch. A freelancer who cannot describe milestones, revisions and what happens if scope changes is as much of a risk as an agency that cannot name the developer who will actually touch your code.
  5. Read the guide on how to hire a solo WordPress freelancer before you commit either way — most of what protects you from a bad agency hire also protects you from a bad freelance one.

If your project is one WordPress site for one business, with a normal timeline, a freelancer with the right background covers it end to end for less money and a shorter chain between you and the work. That is the case for most of what I get asked about. If you want to talk through your specific scope, that is exactly what the WordPress development page and a direct message are for.
