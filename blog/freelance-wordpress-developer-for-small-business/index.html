Freelance WordPress Developer for Small Business: What You Actually Need

Most small business enquiries I get describe a much bigger website than the business actually needs. Here is what genuinely moves the needle, and what you can skip.

ON THIS PAGE

  2. Custom build vs theme vs page builder
  3. Budgeting realistically
  4. Ongoing costs nobody mentions upfront
  5. Questions to ask before you sign

A recurring pattern in small business enquiries: a five-page site described with the scope of a twenty-page one, because a competitor's site or a template demo set the reference point rather than what the business actually needs to do — get found, explain what it sells, and make it easy to get in touch or buy. Everything past that is optional, and optional things cost money whether or not they earn it back.

What most small business sites actually need

  • A homepage that says what you do in one sentence, not a hero slider cycling through five messages nobody reads past the first.
  • Service or product pages with enough detail to answer the obvious questions — price range, process, timeline — before someone has to ask.
  • A contact path that actually gets used. A form, a phone number, and for a lot of businesses now, WhatsApp. Fewer options, used consistently, beat five channels nobody checks.
  • Speed and basic technical SEO. A fast, correctly indexed five-page site outperforms a slow fifteen-page one on every search that matters, almost every time.
  • Mobile-first, genuinely, not "responsive" as a checkbox. Most local and small business traffic is mobile; if that experience is an afterthought, so is most of your traffic.

What is usually optional at launch: blog infrastructure nobody has a plan to write for, a members' area, multi-language support for a single-market business, and animation that exists to look impressive rather than to clarify anything. All of these can be added later, on a working foundation, for less than the cost of building them in from day one on guesswork.

Custom build vs theme vs page builder

Three real options for a small business WordPress site, and the right one depends on who maintains it after launch:

  • A premium theme, lightly customised. Fastest to launch, cheapest, and fine for a straightforward brochure site. The trade-off is you are working within someone else's structure, which shows up as friction the moment you need something the theme was not built for.
  • Elementor or a similar page builder, built properly. Genuinely good for a team that wants to edit the site themselves without touching code. The failure mode is a builder used carelessly — nested containers stacked five deep, which is the single most common cause of a slow WordPress site I get asked to fix.
  • A custom-coded theme. More upfront cost, but the fastest possible load times and no constraints from someone else's plugin decisions. Worth it when performance and a specific design are both non-negotiable; overkill for a five-page site with a modest budget.

For most small businesses, a page builder built by someone who understands performance — not a stack of default blocks — is the right middle ground. It is also what most of the portfolio work here is built on.

Budgeting realistically

The honest range for a well-built small business WordPress site, custom-fitted rather than templated as-is, runs roughly from the low hundreds for a very simple single-page site to a few thousand dollars for a multi-page site with WooCommerce or booking functionality. The full cost breakdown by platform has the actual numbers broken out by scope rather than a single misleading average.

The biggest budget mistake is not spending too much or too little — it is spending on the wrong thing. A business with no clear offer or clear next step for a visitor will not fix that with a bigger budget or more pages. Get the message right on paper before commissioning the build; a developer can make a clear message look good, but cannot invent one from a vague brief.

Ongoing costs nobody mentions upfront

  • Hosting. Cheap shared hosting is where most of the "why is my site slow" complaints start. Budget for hosting that actually fits WordPress, not the lowest number on a comparison table.
  • Plugin and core updates. WordPress needs maintaining. Either you learn to do this safely, or you budget a small monthly retainer for someone who does — the cost of not doing it is usually a broken site at the worst possible time.
  • A domain and SSL renewal, which is small but easy to let lapse if nobody owns the task.
  • Content updates. A site that never changes after launch slowly stops representing the business accurately. Budget occasional update time, even if it is small.

None of this is expensive in isolation. It becomes a problem only when nobody accounted for it, and the first anyone hears about it is when something breaks.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. What is included in this quote, and what is billed separately afterward?
  2. Who owns hosting and maintenance once the site is live — me, or is that a separate service?
  3. Can I edit the site myself afterward, and will I be shown how?
  4. What happens if my business changes and I need a page added in six months?
