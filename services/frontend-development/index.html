THE PROBLEM

What I actually get called in to fix

Alongside my own client work I spent four years leading React front ends at Peymynt and a year building Nuxt.js applications at Sumashtech. That is the background I bring to product teams now: I am comfortable owning the frontend layer of an application, not just styling pages. Most of what makes a front end good is unglamorous — the state that does not get tangled, the component that is genuinely reusable, the layout that holds at 320 pixels, the interaction that still works with a keyboard.

The build does not match the design

Approximated spacing, an invented type scale, hover states nobody implemented. The design was the expensive part; it is worth building accurately. I work from the Figma file directly and raise responsive problems before building, not after.

It is fast on your laptop and slow for users

Oversized bundles, render-blocking scripts, images shipped at full resolution, layout that shifts while loading. Real users are on mid-range phones and imperfect networks, and that is the condition worth optimising for.

It is unusable with a keyboard or a screen reader

Div elements standing in for buttons, focus states removed for looking untidy, forms with no labels. Accessibility is a legal requirement in a growing number of markets and it is far cheaper to build in than to retrofit.

Nobody wants to touch the component

A component that has grown a dozen boolean props and three special cases is one everybody works around instead of using. Keeping components small and honest is what keeps a codebase moving.

PROCESS

How a project runs

  1. Design and technical review

    I go through the design file before writing anything, and flag the places that will not survive a narrow viewport or a long string of real content. Catching these in review costs minutes; catching them in QA costs days.

  2. Component architecture

    What is a component, what is a page, what is shared, and where state actually belongs. Deciding this deliberately is the difference between a codebase that scales and one that calcifies.

  3. Build

    Semantic HTML first, then styling, then behaviour — so the thing works before it is decorated. Mobile-first, tested at real breakpoints on real devices rather than in a browser resize.

  4. Accessibility and performance pass

    Keyboard navigation, focus order, screen reader labels and colour contrast, alongside bundle size, code splitting and Core Web Vitals. A dedicated stage with its own checklist.

  5. Handover

    Readable code, a clear commit history, and documentation of anything non-obvious. The measure of a good handover is that the next developer does not need to ask me anything.

FAQ

Questions I get asked

What people ask before bringing in a frontend developer, answered up front rather than in the first call, including starting prices across the platforms I work on.

What frontend frameworks do you work with?

React and Next.js, Vue and Nuxt.js, and plain JavaScript where a framework would be overhead. Four years of that was leading React work at Peymynt and a year building Nuxt.js applications at Sumashtech, so both are production experience rather than side-project familiarity.

Can you work with our existing team and codebase?

Yes — that is a large part of what I did across my years on agency and product teams. I follow the conventions already in the codebase rather than importing my own preferences, and I would rather ask than guess about anything architectural.

Do you do backend work as well?

No, and I would rather be clear about that than stretch. I work at the frontend layer and integrate with whatever API your backend team provides. For WordPress and Webflow projects I handle the full build, because the backend there is the platform.

Can you convert a Figma design to code?

Yes, and it is a significant share of my work. I build to the file accurately, including hover and focus states, empty states and error states — the parts that are easy to skip and obvious when they are missing.

Do you write tests?

If the project has a testing setup, I work within it. If you want one established, that is worth scoping explicitly rather than assuming — it changes the estimate and it is better discussed than discovered.

Are you available for contract or long-term work?

Both. I take fixed-scope projects and ongoing part-time arrangements with product teams. I am freelance full-time, so availability is a scheduling question rather than a moonlighting one.
